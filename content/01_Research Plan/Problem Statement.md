## Context

[[National Innovation Systems]] (NIS) are traditionally conceptualized in literature as macro-level, institutional frameworks driven by policy, funding streams, and structural triple-helix alliances between state, industry, and academia. Within this ecosystem, [[Universities of Applied Sciences]] (UAS) are positioned as operational nodes, tasked with translating these national strategies into concrete economic and societal value through publicly and privately funded Research, Development, and Innovation (RDI) projects. Standard innovation and economic theories and the innovation [[Model of Diffusion]] treat the NIS as a cohesive, formal, pre-existing structure, a stable "[[Punctualisation|black box]]" that seamlessly funnels macro-policy down to micro-level practice.

## The Problem

This macro-centric perspective creates a theoretical and empirical blind spot. By treating the NIS as a singular, unified entity, traditional frameworks ignore how the innovation system is [[Sociomateriality|sociomaterially]] enacted through practices. When viewed through a [[Praxiography|praxiographic]] and [[Actor-Network Theory]] (ANT) lens and the innovation [[Model of Interessement]], the NIS does not just exist, it must be continuously performed. In modern RDI practice, this performance is heavily mediated by digital environments, such as Microsoft Teams, asynchronous email threads, spreadsheets and complex project application platforms.

Because these practices are scattered across fragmented digital spaces and institutional boundaries, the innovation system becomes [[Multiplicity|multiple]]. It is enacted differently when a coordinator drafts a work package on a shared document versus when an administrator calculates a budget matrix. The problem is that we currently lack an understanding of how these different, localized versions of the innovation system are coordinated, and what socio-material acts successfully allow a loose network of digital traces to stabilize and cross the threshold into becoming an independent, institutionalized RDI project entity, i.e. how it becomes a "black box" or a "creature".

## Rationale

If the "micro-level", socio-material enactments of RDI project preparation remain unexamined, [[Innovation Policy]] remains fundamentally decoupled from the realities of [[Practice]]. Policymakers and university managers will continue to design funding instruments based on an idealized, singular concept of the NIS. This leaves the system vulnerable to commercially unaligned projects, unseen frictions, digital dispersion and coordination failures within the actual networks that are expected to realize national innovation goals.

## Research Objective

This study addresses this gap by tracing an international Horizon Europe project preparation case coordinated by the Lapland University of Applied Sciences. By analyzing the digital traces, this research explores how the National Innovation System is socio-materially [[Enactment|enacted]], how its inherent [[Multiplicity]] is coordinated across different sites of practice and how a project network eventually stabilizes into its own distinct entity, i.e. is "black-boxed".

### Research Questions

- **Q1: How is the National Innovation System socio-materially enacted by University of Applied Science through a publicly-funded RDI project?**
  - Q1.1: What is the actor-network of a project preparation?
  - Q1.2: How is the innovation system enacted differently across different sites of practice?
  - Q1.3: How is the multiplicity of the innovation system coordinated?
  - Q1.4 What are the acts leading to the creation of a project as it's own entity?

## Scope and Demarcation

This study is empirically bounded to the project preparation phase of a single funding proposal coordinated. Socio-materially, the network is "cut" at the digital perimeter of the preparation team’s collaborative environments and its digital traces. The primary empirical scope is the socio-material practices, specifically asynchronous email threads, Microsoft Teams interactions, and collaborative digital document workspaces generated by the actor-network during this pre-award window. To maintain analytical depth, several areas are explicitly demarcated outside the boundaries of this research:

- Temporal: This study focuses exclusively on the genesis and preparation of the proposal. It does not track the post-award implementation, execution, or long-term reporting phases of the resulting RDI project.
- Analytical: In strict alignment with the principles of generalized symmetry in Actor-Network Theory and praxiography, this research does not investigate the internal cognitive motivations, psychological states or unrecorded intentions of the human participants. The analytical focus is bounded entirely to how the innovation system is visibly enacted through relational practices and material digital artifacts.
- Macro-Policy: While this study leverages the NIS as a core theoretical lens, it does not aim to provide a macro-level evaluation of national innovation policy effectiveness, nor does it offer a comparative institutional analysis of different universities. Instead, it is bounded to the localized, multiple performances of the innovation system as it is coordinated within specific digital environments.

## Contribution and Relevance

### Theoretical

The study contributes to innovation theory by re-conceptualizing the NIS not as a static pre-existing macro-structure, but as a continuous socio-material performance and achievement. By pairing NIS with a praxiographic Actor-Network Theory lens, this research provides a novel theoretical vocabulary to explain how macro-level political mandates are translated into existence through micro-level digital practices. It advances ANT scholarship by directly demonstrating how the concept of multiplicity operates within highly bureaucratic, multi-institutional funding regimes.

### Methodological

Methodologically, this study offers a blueprint for conducting relational ethnography within contemporary hybrid multi-site academic workspaces. Rather than relying on retrospective interviews this research utilizes direct digital traces as active empirical sites. It contributes to the "following of the actors" symmetrically through digital environments, treating software architecture and templates not as passive tools, but as active co-shapers of research design.

### Practical

There are practical stakes for innovation management concerning all the actors in the publicly funded RDI activities, particularly the UAS's, SME's and the funding bodies. By "opening the black box" of project preparation, this study examines the digital friction and flow and the coordination costs that human actors face when trying to align disparate institutional networks. It examines how bureaucratic templates and digital infrastructures actively transform the innovation policies they are meant to implement. It may also provide actionable insights into how digital environments can be better designed or navigated to support complex proposal assemblies.
