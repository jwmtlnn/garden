> [!quote] [[Bruno Latour]]
> ... _any thing_ that does modify a state of affairs by making a difference is an actor ... [[@latourReassemblingSocialIntroduction2005]]p.71).

## Introduction & general definition

Terms _actor_ and _actant_ both refer to **entities with the capacity to act**. The "actant/actor" are used sometimes interchangeably, but the distinction can also be a key tool in ANT for analyzing the unfolding of [[Agency]] [[@matozziWhatCanANT2019]]p.94). These entities participating in the action are not strictly (intentional) humans, but extends to include the "nonhumans", i.e. anything that makes a difference [[@latourReassemblingSocialIntroduction2005]]p.71-72).

Crucially for ANT, the term actor in "[[Actor-Network]]" is intentionally oxymoronic, embodying the tension between a centered "actor" and a decentered "network", thus eliding the distinction between "agency" and "structure" [[@lawAntComplexityNaming1999]]p.5). A defining aspect for both of them in ANT is that they make a difference. If an entity makes no difference, it is not considered an actor but a passive [[Intermediaries and Mediators|intermediary]]. An actor is thus what is not entirely substitutable. [[@latourReassemblingSocialIntroduction2005]]p.130;153.)

## Actant/Actor

"Actant" is often used when the entity does not yet have a specific figuration or identity beyond its capacity to act [[@latourReassemblingSocialIntroduction2005]]p.71). Generally, it is used to point to "anything that acts" regardless of its [[Ontology|ontological]] status, size, scale, or features, providing a descriptive term for the principle of symmetry [[@matozziWhatCanANT2019]]p.94). Actant is then a slightly broader term than actor, and often used in a more general and symmetrical way to include anything that participates in a network and influences outcomes, particularly before a stable identity or figuration is established.

"Actor" can then denote an actant with more specific features, relationships, and a recognized role within the network. Actor can be understood as an actant that possesses additional features and engages in various relations beyond simply performing an action. While anything that acts is an actant, what acts is not reducible solely to the performed action. For example [[@akrichSummaryConvenientVocabulary1992]], a written note and a weight can both function as the same actant by reminding a hotel guest to return the key. However, in the given example they are different actors with distinct physical characteristics and may contribute differently to the hotel manager's goals.

## Figuration

Figuration gives actants "flesh and features that make them have some form or shape, no matter how vague". These are not limited to anthropomorphic forms; they can also be e.g. ideo- or technomorphic. An actor is an actant that has been given a specific figuration. In ANT process, the analysis often involves moving from the identification of actants to the figuration of actors with more defined roles and attributes within a network. Therefore, the figuration that distinguishes the actor from the actant is one that endows the actant with specific characteristics, attributes agency to it, or positions it within a network in a defined way, moving beyond the basic definition of "anything that acts". [[@latourReassemblingSocialIntroduction2005]]p.53-55.)

## Symmetry & Agency

ANT treats actors symmetrically by employing a methodological principle known as [[Generalized Symmetry]]. This principle suggests that when studying a situation or a network, researchers should remain agnostic regarding what counts as an actor and should not a priori assume that humans and nonhumans have fundamentally different types of agency or causal power. [[@lawMethodMessSocial2004]]p.101-103;[[@callonElementsSociologyTranslation1986]]p.201.)

ANT also proposes that [[Agency]] is not an inherent property of individual actors (whether human or nonhuman) but rather an emergent effect of the relationships and associations within a network [[@nimmoActorNetworkTheoryMethodology2011]]p.112). Actors are seen as "network effects" because they do not possess inherent, fixed properties or agency in isolation. Instead, their characteristics, capabilities, and even their very existence are consequences of the relationships and connections they form and maintain within a heterogeneous network of other actors/actants - human and nonhuman. [[@lawActorNetworkTheory1999]];[[@lawAntComplexityNaming1999]].
